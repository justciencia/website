## My story

I'm a first-generation Mexican American with a PhD in chemical engineering from Stanford University, and I created justciencia to celebrate first-generation, low-income scientists and engineers and to help everyone understand the science they do.

## The mission

justciencia celebrates scientists and engineers powered by their roots: the families and communities who shaped them and cheered them on. We honor not only their discoveries but the long, often unseen paths that led them here. Each profile translates their research into plain language, using analogies drawn from everyday life, so the public can understand the science and see themselves in the people who do it. Free, bilingual, and created by a first-generation Mexican American PhD.

### What we mean by first-generation, low-income

When we say *first-generation, low-income*, we mean a scientist who was the first in their family to complete a four-year college degree and who grew up with limited resources or in poverty. We feature scientists of any background who share that experience, and each scientist decides how much of their own story to tell.

## How we write

**Plain language first.** We explain a term the first time it appears, choose a simple word when one will do, and never treat a reader as someone who "just doesn't get it." The explainers, graph guides, and paper summaries in [Stories]({{ '/stories/' | relative_url }}) follow the same approach.

**Analogies from everyday life.** A good analogy lets you feel an idea before you learn its name. A few examples of how we think about it:

- A membrane that lets only certain ions through is like the doorman at a quinceañera who only lets in the guests on the list.
- A catalyst is like the comadre who knows the shortcut through the mercado. You still get from home to the stall, but with far less effort, and she isn't used up by the trip.
- An electric current is like water moving through the acequias that irrigate a field. The voltage is the push, and the current is how much water flows.

## Our process

Scientists review and approve their profiles in writing before anything is published.

1. We talk with the scientist, or they share their story in writing.
2. We draft the profile in plain language, including the path that led them here and the science itself.
3. The scientist reads the full draft and asks for any changes.
4. The scientist approves it in writing. An email is enough.
5. Only then do we publish it.
